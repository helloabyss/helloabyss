## THE ANGLE

The channel's whole thesis is that revolutions arrive when **cost per unit collapses** —
Watt's condenser, the Model T at $260. This video is the same lens pointed at a company moving
**the other way**.

Moore's Law was never a promise about speed. Moore's 1965 paper was titled *"Cramming more
components onto integrated circuits"* and its subject was, in his own words, the **minimum cost
per component**. Two promises were bundled: more transistors, and *cheaper* transistors. The
first still holds. **The second has stopped.** A TSMC 2nm wafer costs ~$30,000 against
~$25,000–27,000 for 3nm, a 2nm chip platform costs ~$725M to develop, and an advanced fab runs
$15–20B.

That is why this is a finance story and not a gadget story — and it fits PARADOCS10X exactly:
*watch the cost, not the product.*

### ✅ FORK A confirmed — prices rose
Apple raised Pro pricing by **exactly $100**. Fork B (prices held) was discarded.

Two things the keynote handed the thesis for free:
1. **Reporting on the increase kept returning to rising component costs** — the video's argument,
   corroborated by the coverage rather than asserted over it.
2. **Both numbers landed below the fear.** Analysts had floated $200–350; it came in at $100.
   The wafer premium was feared at ~50%; it came in at 10–20%. That symmetry became a new beat,
   and it is what keeps the video from overclaiming: this is a squeeze, not a catastrophe.

**[5 — MOORE, PRECISELY]**
> In April 1965, an engineer named Gordon Moore published a piece in Electronics magazine
> called "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits." He had five data points, from
> 1959 to 1964, and he drew a line through them. That line is the most famous prediction in
> the history of technology. And almost everybody remembers it wrong.

**[6 — WHAT IT ACTUALLY SAID]**
> Moore did not predict that chips would get faster. Read the subtitle of his own article: with
> unit cost falling as the number of components rises. What Moore was plotting was the number
> of components you could put on a chip **at minimum cost per component**. Moore's Law was
> never a law of physics. It was an observation about **economics**.

**[7 — THE TWO PROMISES]**
> And inside it were two separate promises. One: the number of transistors would keep doubling.
> Two: each of those transistors would get **cheaper**. For roughly fifty years, both were
> true at the same time, and that combination is the single most powerful economic force of the
> twentieth century. It is why a calculator that cost a fortune in 1970 is now given away free
> inside something else.

**[8 — THE DIVIDEND]**
> Think about what that dividend paid for. Every generation, you got more computer for less
> money, without anyone having to be clever about pricing. Companies could add features and
> hold prices. Whole industries — personal computers, mobile phones, the internet itself — were
> built on the assumption that the hardware underneath them would keep getting cheaper on its
> own. Nobody had to plan for the alternative.

**[9 — WHERE IT STARTS TO BEND]**
> Then, somewhere in the last decade or so, the second promise started to fail. The transistors
> kept shrinking. They stopped getting cheaper. And the reason is not mysterious. It is the cost
> of the machine that makes them.

**[10 — THE FAB]**
> A leading-edge chip factory now costs somewhere between fifteen and twenty **billion**
> dollars to build. The lithography tools inside it, which print circuits using extreme
> ultraviolet light, are among the most expensive manufacturing objects ever made. Those are
> fixed costs, and they have been rising faster than the number of transistors they produce.

**[11 — THE DESIGN COST]**
> And it isn't only the factory. Designing a single chip platform on a two-nanometre process
> now runs to roughly **seven hundred and twenty-five million dollars** — and that is for a
> family of chips, not one part. That number is a wall. It means the list of companies that can
> even attempt a leading-edge chip is now a handful, worldwide.

**[12 — THE WAFER]**
> Which brings us to the number this whole video is about. A two-nanometre wafer from TSMC —
> the Taiwanese company that manufactures Apple's processors — reportedly costs around **thirty
> thousand dollars**. The three-nanometre wafer it replaces runs about twenty-five to
> twenty-seven thousand. That's a premium of roughly ten to twenty percent, for the same piece
> of silicon, one generation on.

**[13 — THE HINGE]**
> Do you see the pattern break yet? For fifty years, the next node meant *cheaper*. Moore drew
> his line through minimum cost per component. Now the next node means **more expensive**, and
> you are looking at the first consumer product where somebody has to decide who pays for that.
> The transistor count still doubles. The dividend is gone.

**[18 — WHY THAT COMPOUNDS]**

> capacity is effectively unlimited and supply is five fabs, the price does not need a

> assembly, distribution and tariffs all move too, and some of them move more. Yields improve

**[22 — THE REBUTTAL]**
> But notice what none of those objections do. Not one of them restores the old promise. The
> best case here is that transistors get more expensive *slowly*. For fifty years the industry
> got a discount every generation and built its entire business model on it. The argument now
> is only about how fast the bill grows. That is a different world, and the products built for
> the old one have not caught up to it.

**[23 — THREE SIGNALS TO WATCH]**
> So here are three signals to watch, instead of the headlines. Signal one: Apple's **gross
> margin** in the next quarterly report. If prices rose and margin held, the cost was passed to

> whole video is asking. Signal two: the price of the standard iPhone when it arrives in spring.
> That's the true test — the Pro can absorb anything, the cheap phone can't. Signal three:
> whether anyone other than Apple and the AI companies ships a two-nanometre product at all.
> If the answer is no, the node stopped being a mass-market technology.

**[24 — WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU]**
> And what does it mean for you? Three things. If you're buying, the upgrade cycle is
> lengthening for a structural reason, not a marketing one — the gap between generations is
> getting smaller while the price gap gets bigger. If you're invested in this industry, the
> companies that win are the ones that own the manufacturing or can pass costs through, not the
> ones in the middle. And if you're building anything on top of computing, stop assuming the
> hardware underneath you gets cheaper by itself. That assumption held for fifty years. It is

The ten-year Treasury yield just hit five point one two percent. That is the highest since 2007, and most of it happened in a single day.

The ten-year is what it costs the government to borrow for a decade. Almost every other price in finance is set off the back of it.

Start with the trigger. A seventy billion dollar auction of five-year notes drew weak demand. When buyers step back, the government pays up, and the whole curve reprices.

Here is why your portfolio felt it. A Treasury is about as close to risk-free as markets get. At five percent, it competes with stocks for the same money, and stocks have to promise more to win it.

The companies that hurt most are the ones whose profits sit furthest in the future. Data centres, chips and power are all built with borrowed money, so a higher rate discounts them harder.

It is not only markets. The thirty-year mortgage hit seven point zero three percent this week, above seven for the first time in about twenty months. That is the same move arriving at your kitchen table.

One correction, because the version going around gets this wrong. It says the same thing happened in August 2024. It did not. Yields fell that month, from four point two eight to three point seven three percent. The last time the ten-year touched five was October 2023.

So watch the ten-year, not the headline. It quietly sets your mortgage, the cost of corporate debt, and what the market will pay for growth.

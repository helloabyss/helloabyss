### 0 · Cold open (0:00)
[G0: title card + "Educational only. Not financial advice." on screen by 0:04]

You opened a Fidelity account. You logged in. And you got a wall of numbers, a row of
tabs, and three different kinds of cash.

In the next ten minutes I'll walk through every section of that screen, how cash actually
turns into a stock, and what happens to your money after you sell — including the one
mistake that can get your account restricted for ninety days.

This is educational only, not financial advice, and I'm not affiliated with Fidelity.
Let's open it up.

### 1 · The map (≈0:35)
[Shot 1 — Accounts & Trade menu → Portfolio → account list → tabs]

Everything lives under **Accounts & Trade**, then **Portfolio**. On the left is your list
of accounts — a brokerage account, maybe a Roth IRA. Click one, and the page switches to
that account.

The tabs you'll use most are **Summary, Positions, Activity & Orders, Balances,** and
**Documents.** Fidelity moves things around now and then, so if your labels look a little
different, the ideas are the same.

Summary is the snapshot — total value, and how much it moved today. Useful, but it hides
the detail. The detail is in Positions.

### 2 · Positions (≈1:20)
[Shot 2 — Positions tab, slow pass left → right across the column headers]

Positions is a table. One row for each thing you own. Read it left to right.

**Symbol** — the ticker.
**Last price** — the most recent trade. During market hours it updates as you watch.
**Today's gain/loss** — how much that position moved since yesterday's close, in dollars
and percent.
**Total gain/loss** — the big one. What it's worth now, minus what you paid.
**Current value** — shares times last price.
**Percent of account** — how many of your eggs are in this basket.
**Quantity** — how many shares. Yes, it can show decimals.
**Cost basis** — what you paid, as a total and as an average per share.

Here's what most beginners miss. Every gain on this page is **unrealized.** It's a paper
number. It only becomes real — and only becomes taxable — when you sell.

Now look for the line that isn't a stock. That's your cash. Fidelity calls it the
**core position.**

### 3 · The core position — your wallet (≈2:30)
[Shot 3 — click the cash/core line in Positions] [G1: money-flow diagram, part 1]

The core position is your account's wallet. Money you deposit lands here. Money from
every sale lands here. And every purchase is paid from here.

It isn't always plain cash. Depending on your account it can be a government money market
fund — like SPAXX — or a bank deposit sweep, or a Fidelity cash option. They pay different
rates. Click the cash line to see which one you have.

Getting money in: you link your bank and send an electronic transfer. Fidelity says these
generally process in one to three business days.

But here's the wrinkle. If you send it before 4 p.m. Eastern on a business day, Fidelity
lets you **trade** with up to twenty-five thousand dollars of it the same day — while it
stays on hold for **withdrawals.** Which is why one account can show two very different
cash numbers. That's the Balances tab.

### 4 · Balances — the two cash numbers (≈3:30)
[Shot 4 — Balances tab, hold on the two lines] [G2: trade-vs-withdraw meters]

Open Balances. For now, ignore everything except two lines.

**Cash available to trade** is what you can put into investments right now without adding
money.

**Cash available to withdraw** is money that has fully cleared and can go back to your bank.

They're often different. Say you have a thousand dollars of settled cash, and on Monday you
sell five hundred dollars of stock. The moment that sale goes through, cash available to
trade jumps to fifteen hundred. Cash available to withdraw stays at a thousand. The five
hundred only counts for withdrawal on the **settlement date.**

Which brings us to the most important word on this platform: settlement. Let's see it in a
real trade.

### 5 · Buying a stock — turning cash into shares (≈4:30)
[Shot 5 — Trade ticket, field by field. STOP AT PREVIEW.] [G3: order types card]

Click **Trade.** I'm going to fill the ticket and stop at Preview, so no real order goes in.

**Account** — pick the one your cash is in.
**Symbol** — type the ticker. A quote appears: the bid, the ask, the last price.
**Action** — Buy.
**Quantity** — whole shares, or switch to dollars. Fidelity lets you buy slices of US
stocks and ETFs starting at one dollar.

**Order type.** This is where people lose money by accident.
A **market** order buys at the best price available right now. It usually fills fast, but
you don't control the price.
A **limit** order buys only at your price or better. You control the price — but it might
never fill.

**Time in force.** A **Day** order expires at the close if it hasn't filled.
**Good 'til canceled** stays open until it fills or you cancel it — at Fidelity, up to
180 days.

Now **Preview.** Read the estimated cost and check it against your cash available to
trade. Only then would you click Place order.

When you place it, your cash available to trade drops right away. When it fills, a new row
appears in Positions. And the trade **settles one business day later** — T plus one, the
US rule since May 2024. That's when the money actually leaves your core position.

### 6 · Selling — what happens after (≈6:00)
[Shot 6 — Trade ticket with Action = Sell, STOP AT PREVIEW] [G1 part 2 + G4: GFV timeline]

Selling is the same ticket with Action set to **Sell.** All your shares, some of them, or a
dollar amount.

Here's what happens after the sale goes through:
One — the shares leave Positions.
Two — the money lands in your core position, **unsettled.**
Three — you can spend it right away. Cash available to trade includes it immediately.
Four — you can't withdraw it until it settles, the next business day.
And five — your gain or loss is now **realized.** That matters for tax.

Now the trap. In a regular cash account you're allowed to buy with unsettled money. But if
you then **sell what you bought before that money settles,** that's a **good faith
violation.**

Example. Monday morning you sell Stock A. At lunch you use that money to buy Stock B —
totally fine. Monday afternoon B jumps, and you sell it. That's the violation. You sold B
before it was paid for with settled cash.

Three of those in twelve months, and Fidelity restricts the account for ninety days — you
can only buy with settled cash.

The fix is simple: if you bought with unsettled money, hold the new position until the
original sale settles.

### 7 · Taxes after a sale (≈7:30)
[Shot 7 — Documents tab, hover over tax forms] [G5: tax clock]

In a regular taxable account, selling at a gain creates a tax bill. Inside an IRA, buying
and selling generally isn't taxed — the rules apply when you withdraw.

How a gain is taxed depends on how long you held it. **One year or less** is short-term,
taxed like your paycheck. **More than a year** is long-term, which gets lower rates.

After the year ends, your **Form 1099-B** shows up under Documents. It lists every sale,
what you got for it, and whether it was short- or long-term. That's what goes to whoever
does your taxes.

### 8 · Activity & Orders (≈8:15)
[Shot 8 — Activity & Orders: open orders, then history, click one trade]

Last tab: **Activity & Orders.** Open orders sit here until they fill — this is where you
cancel a limit order you no longer want. Below that is history: every buy, sell, deposit
and dividend, with dates. Click any trade to see the price you actually got. If something
in your balance looks wrong, this is where you find out why.

### 9 · The honest part (≈8:50)
[G6: two warnings]

Two honest warnings before you go.

First — a **stop loss** order doesn't guarantee your price. When it triggers it becomes a
market order, and in a fast drop it can fill well below your stop.

Second — knowing the platform makes you competent, not profitable. The ticket puts every
order one click away. Including the bad ones.

### 10 · Close (≈9:20)
[Shot 9 — back to Balances] [G7: end card + disclaimer]

Here's your homework. Log in tonight, open Balances, and write down two numbers: cash
available to trade and cash available to withdraw. If they're different, you now know
exactly why.

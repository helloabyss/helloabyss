## 1. The topic, in one sentence

Starting with the 2026 tax year, if your **2025 Social Security wages from your current
employer exceeded $150,000**, your 401(k) catch-up contribution must be **Roth** — and the test
is not your salary and not your taxable income, it is W-2 **Box 3**, which your own pre-tax
401(k) deferrals do **not** reduce, so people who believe they are under the line are over it.

**Why it fits the proven shape:** a named thing (the SECURE 2.0 Roth catch-up rule / the
$150,000 wage test), one hard number ($150,000, or the $8,000 catch-up), and the cost that
number hides (the deduction is gone this year — and if the plan has no Roth feature, the
catch-up itself is gone). The correction is actionable and costs nothing to act on: look at
Box 3 on your 2025 W-2, not your salary.

**Decay estimate:** **~14 months.** This is a statutory change that has already taken effect;
the mechanism does not rot. The *figures* are annual — $150,000, $24,500, $8,000, $11,250 are
**2026** values and all go stale when the IRS publishes 2027 limits (expected ~Nov 2026).
**Hard stop: re-verify every dollar figure after 2026-11-01.**

### Recorded conflicts

- **C1 — $145,000 vs $150,000.** Several otherwise-credible law-firm alerts (Lathrop GPM,
  Baker Donelson, Groom, Trucker Huss, ThinkAdvisor) still print **$145,000** for 2026. They
  were written **before** the IRS released the 2026 limits on 2025-11-13 and reflect the
  un-indexed statutory base. Sources published after that date (CPA Journal, Vorys, Paylocity,
  RSM, CAPTRUST) all print **$150,000**, and Vorys explicitly calls the increase "unexpected,"
  which explains why the stale figure is still so widely cited. **Resolution: use $150,000**,
  and say on screen that it applies to 2025 wages. **Do not use $145,000.** This is the single
  most likely way this video gets a number wrong.
- **C2 — Box 3 vs Box 5.** Most sources say **Box 3** (Social Security wages). Some note Box 3
  is capped at the wage base, so Box 5 (Medicare wages, uncapped) may be used for very high
  earners. For the $150,000 test the distinction does not bite, because the 2025 Box 3 cap is
  $176,100 — above the threshold. **Scriptwriter: say "Social Security wages," not "Box 3,"**
  unless the frame has room for the caveat.
- **C3 — "effective 2026" vs "regs apply 2027."** Not a real contradiction, but it is reported
  as one. The **statutory** requirement bites 2026-01-01 (the Notice 2023-62 transition relief
  ran out and the final regs did not extend it). The **regulations'** formal applicability date
  is tax years beginning after 2026-12-31, with reasonable good-faith compliance allowed for
  2026. **Scriptwriter: say "this applies to your 2026 catch-up." Do not say "the IRS rules
  take effect in 2026"** — that is the formulation that is wrong.
- **C4 — final-reg date.** Sept 14, Sept 15 and Sept 16 2025 all appear. The Federal Register
  publication date is **2025-09-16**. **Keep the date off screen**; it adds nothing.

## 4. Counter-evidence — where the thesis is weak

This section is mandatory and some of it should reach the frame.

1. **It is not a tax increase. It is a timing shift, and it may be a gain.** The deduction is
   lost this year, but Roth contributions *and all their earnings* come out tax-free under
   qualifying rules. For someone who expects a similar or higher rate in retirement, forced Roth
   is a win, not a penalty. Several practitioners quoted in [Thomson
   Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/prepping-for-mandatory-roth-catch-up-contributions-next-year/)
   and [Foley](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/03/mandatory-roth-catch-up-more-meets-eye/)
   make exactly this point. **A script that frames this as "you lost $2,560" is wrong.** The
   honest framing is cash-flow and surprise, not loss. *(Grade: HIGH on the structure, which is
   definitional.)*
2. **It does not touch most people — by a wide margin.** Only **17%** of catch-up-eligible
   participants make catch-up contributions at all (row 16), and of those only the ones over
   the wage line at a single employer are affected. The real audience is a slice of a slice.
   This is the strongest disconfirming number in the file and it belongs in the video.
3. **It does not touch:** anyone under 50; anyone whose 2025 Box 3 wages at that employer were
   $150,000 or less; 2026 new hires at a new employer; the self-employed with no W-2 wages;
   and anyone who simply contributes under the $24,500 standard limit (rows 18–21).
4. **The actual worst outcome is not tax, it is the lockout.** A plan without a Roth feature
   that does not amend leaves affected high earners **unable to make any catch-up at all** —
   they lose $8,000 (or $11,250) of tax-advantaged space entirely, not just the deduction
   (row 9). That is a larger harm than the deduction and it is the part retail does not know.
5. **Where the "Box 3 ≠ salary" hook is weaker than it sounds.** The gap between salary and
   Box 3 is created mainly by pre-tax 401(k) deferrals, which *add* to Box 3 relative to Box 1
   taxable income — but Section 125 cafeteria-plan deductions (health premiums, FSA) *do*
   reduce Box 3. So the two effects push in opposite directions, and for some people Box 3 is
   lower than salary, not higher. **Do not say "Box 3 is always higher than you think."** Say
   the test is not the number you think it is, and point at the W-2.
6. **The threshold indexes.** $150,000 is not a permanent line. It rose from $145,000 for the
   first time this cycle and will likely move again for 2027.
